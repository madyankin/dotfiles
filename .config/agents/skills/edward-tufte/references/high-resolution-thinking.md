# High-resolution thinking

"There is no such thing as information overload. There is only bad design."

When people feel overloaded, the cause is almost always organization, not volume.
Design for the audience's intelligence: layer, enable reading at several levels,
trust them to explore.

## Escaping flatland

| Channel | Adds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Position | 2D location | Scatter x/y |
| Length | 1D magnitude | Bars |
| Color | Category or gradient | Heat map |
| Size | Magnitude | Bubbles |
| Shape | Category | Symbol markers |
| Orientation | Angle, direction | Arrow plots |
| Small multiples | One more dimension per panel | Facets |
| Time | Sequence | Animation, sparingly |
| Layering | Overlaid information | Annotations |

## Macro and micro

Macro: pattern, trend and structure at a glance, from a distance. Micro: specific
values and exceptions, up close. Both must work. Sacrifice neither.

## Workflow

1. **Resist "simplify".** Ask: which information would be lost? Who decided the audience cannot handle it? Is the problem complexity, or organization?
2. **Layer the content.**

| Layer | Content | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Must be seen first | Prominent, high contrast |
| Secondary | Important context | Present, quieter |
| Tertiary | Supporting detail | Found on inspection |
| Reference | Verification, source | Small, in the margins |

3. **Design for density** (data points per square inch). Raise density while keeping clarity.

| Density | Points per sq in | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Low | under 10 | Typical slide |
| Medium | 10–50 | Good business graphic |
| High | 50–100 | Statistical graphic |
| Very high | 100+ | Map, scientific plot |

4. **Separate layers by weight.** Bold for primary data, medium for secondary, light gray for tertiary and reference, white space to group.
5. **Enable close reading.** Real numbers, not just shapes. Direct labels. Annotations at the insights. The source.
6. **Test both levels.** Step back: is the main pattern visible? Lean in: can any specific value be found?

## Document vs slides

| Format | Carries | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Slides | 40–60 words, 1–2 graphics | Entertainment, simple messages |
| Document | 1,000+ words, integrated graphics | Analysis, decisions, records |

The alternative to a deck: write one dense, well-designed document; hand it out
before or at the meeting; let people read in silence; then discuss.

Low resolution: six slides for six metrics, legend apart from the data, transitions
between slides. High resolution: all six on one page, sparklines for trend, direct
labels, comparison context, printable, annotatable.

## Worked example

Input: an executive summary with 12 KPIs; leadership says "too dense, pick the top 3".

The question is not which 3 but how to show 12 clearly. Layer 1: the 3 critical KPIs
as large numbers with sparklines and a status color, top of the page. Layer 2: 6
important KPIs in a 2×3 grid, medium size, small sparklines. Layer 3: 3 supporting
KPIs in one row, current vs prior only, light weight.

One page, all 12, hierarchy guides the eye. "Top 3 only" drops 9 numbers someone
needed, forces follow-ups, and insults readers who parse 50-line financial
statements. Do not reduce information. Organize it.

## Limits

The general public may need more guidance. Safety warnings and emergency
instructions demand simplicity. Density without organization is chaos. Test with
real users.
